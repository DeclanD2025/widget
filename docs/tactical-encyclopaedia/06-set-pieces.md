# 06 — Set Pieces

Set pieces are the **rehearsed, static-start "fifth phase"**. Because they restart from a
known position with time to organise, they are the most *coachable* moments in football —
and now decide a large and growing share of goals. Many clubs employ dedicated set-piece
coaches for exactly this reason.

---

## Why set pieces matter so much

- They are **repeatable**: the same routine can be drilled and reused every match.
- The defence is **static**, so attackers can engineer movement, blocks and mismatches.
- They are a **leveller**: an underdog can beat a superior side on dead balls alone.
- Expected-goals (xG) value from set pieces is high relative to how often they occur.

---

## Attacking Set Pieces

### Corners
Three broad delivery philosophies, often mixed:
- **Inswinger** — curls toward goal; pressures the keeper, good for near-post flicks and
  forcing errors.
- **Outswinger** — curls away from goal; takes the keeper out, good for attackers running
  onto the ball.
- **Short corner** — a pass to change the angle, draw defenders out, and create a crossing
  or shooting position.

Common routines and concepts:
- **Near-post flick-on** — a runner glances the ball across goal for others to attack.
- **Blockers / screens** — legal "picks" that obstruct markers (especially against
  man-marking) to free a target.
- **Stacks and trains** — attackers bunched together then exploding to different posts to
  break zonal and man schemes.
- **Targeting zones** — near post, far post, penalty spot, and the **pull-back/edge** for a
  second-ball striker.

### Free-Kicks
- **Direct (shooting range)** — a shot on goal; teams set the wall and the keeper covers his
  near side, expecting the shot over/around the wall.
  - Routines: a runner in/over the wall, a dummy runner, two-man disguise, a rolled ball to
    a striker.
- **Indirect / crossing free-kicks** — treated like a corner with movement: delivery to a
  zone with rehearsed runs and blocks.
- **Wide free-kicks** — choice between an early whipped delivery and a worked short routine.

### Long Throws
- A specialist long thrower turns a throw-in near the corner into a de-facto cross — no
  offside applies, and it can be as dangerous as a corner for aerially strong teams.

### Penalties
- A 1v1 of preparation: the taker's technique and the keeper's research/positioning.
  Increasingly data-driven (where a taker tends to go, the keeper's dive tendencies).

## Defending Set Pieces

### Three marking systems (same logic as open play)
- **Zonal** — defenders guard *areas*; strong against blocks, can be beaten by a runner
  attacking a zone with momentum.
- **Man-marking** — each defender tracks a runner; vulnerable to screens, blocks and
  rotations that drag markers away.
- **Hybrid** — the common modern setup: a zonal core (protecting the key central areas)
  plus man-markers on the biggest aerial threats.

### Defensive checklist
- **Protect the key zones** — the six-yard box, near post, and penalty spot.
- **Post protection** — a defender on one or both posts (a coaching choice; some keepers
  prefer the extra body, others want the space to attack the cross).
- **Edge-of-box cover** — a man (or two) on the edge to clear the **second ball** and stop
  the cut-back shot.
- **A counter outlet** — leave a quick player high so a cleared set piece becomes a
  counter-attack (turning defence into your own transition).

---

## Set Pieces in match reading

When assessing a fixture (Chapter 10), the **set-piece differential** is its own factor:
- Which team has the better deliverers and aerial targets?
- Which defends dead balls well, and with which system?
- A big edge here can decide an otherwise even, low-chance game — exactly the matches where
  open play won't separate the teams.
