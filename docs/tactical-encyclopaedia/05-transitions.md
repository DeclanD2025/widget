# 05 — Transitions

A transition is the **moment of change** between having and not having the ball. They last
only seconds, but a disproportionate share of goals are scored in them — because for those
few seconds, one team is organised and the other is not.

> If you learn to watch only one thing in a match, watch the **transitions**.

---

## The Two Transitions

| | Attacking transition | Defensive transition |
|---|---------------------|----------------------|
| **Trigger** | You just **won** the ball | You just **lost** the ball |
| **Opponent state** | Disorganised, committed forward | Organised, about to break |
| **Your goal** | Exploit the disorder fast | Stop the break — win it back or delay |
| **The window** | ~3–5 seconds before they re-set | ~3–5 seconds before you re-set |

---

## 1. Attacking Transition (The Counter)

When you win the ball, the opponent is briefly out of shape — players are upfield, the
defensive line may be high, and markers are not yet assigned.

### Principles
- **Vertical first thought.** The first action after winning the ball should look forward.
  A quick, penetrative first pass beats the opponent's recovery; a safe sideways pass wastes
  the window.
- **Speed beats numbers.** A 2v3 counter at full speed can be more dangerous than a 4v4
  attack against a set defence, because the defenders are facing the wrong way.
- **Support the break.** Runners must get *beyond and alongside* the ball-carrier to turn a
  1v2 into a 2v2 or 3v2.
- **Know when to slow down.** If the numerical advantage is gone, the best teams *retain*
  the ball and rebuild rather than forcing a low-percentage attack ("counter, or control").

### Personnel
Counter-attacking teams prize **pace** (to attack the vacated space), a **decisive
passer** to launch the break, and a striker who can **hold or run** depending on the
situation.

---

## 2. Defensive Transition (Losing the Ball)

The instant you lose the ball you have a binary choice, and good teams make it
*collectively* and *immediately*.

### Option A — Counterpress (win it back now)
- Swarm the ball-winner before he can pass or run (the "5-second rule").
- Works only if your attacking shape was compact enough to have bodies near the ball
  (this is why rest attack and counterpressing are linked).
- The reward: regain the ball high, against a defence still facing its own goal — your best
  chance of all.

### Option B — Recover and re-set (fall back)
- If the ball can't be won back immediately, the priority is to **delay** the counter and
  retreat into shape.
- **Delaying actions:** the nearest player slows the ball-carrier (jockeying, not diving
  in) while teammates sprint to recover their positions and protect the centre.
- **The tactical foul** — a deliberate, cynical foul to stop a dangerous counter before it
  starts. A yellow-card risk traded for preventing a clear chance; a recognised (if
  unsporting) part of elite game management.

### The decision rule
Counterpress when you have numbers and compactness around the ball; fall back when you are
outnumbered or stretched. Misjudging this — counterpressing in isolation while outnumbered —
is how teams concede on the break.

---

## 3. Rest Defence & Rest Attack — transition insurance

These two "resting" structures (introduced in Chapters 03–04) exist precisely to win
transitions:

- **Rest defence** — bodies kept behind the ball *while attacking* so a lost ball meets
  immediate pressure and cover (your defensive-transition head start).
- **Rest attack** — attackers kept high *while defending* so a won ball can be attacked
  instantly (your attacking-transition head start).

A team's transition quality is largely *pre-decided* by these resting shapes before the
turnover ever happens.

---

## 4. Turnover Maps — a practical reading tool

When analysing a match, mentally (or literally) map **where each team wins and loses the
ball**:

- **High turnovers won** → a working press and a platform for the best chances.
- **Turnovers lost in your own half** → counter-attacks conceded; a structural problem.
- **Where the opponent counters from** → tells you which of your players/zones is exposed
  in transition.

Two teams can have identical possession stats and completely different games depending on
*where* the ball changes hands. The turnover map, not the possession percentage, explains
the chances.
