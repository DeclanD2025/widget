# 12 — Pressing Schemes in Depth

Chapter 04 introduced pressing. This chapter is the full reference: the *types* of press,
how each is organised against common build-ups, their triggers and weaknesses, and how a
build-up team plays *through* them.

Diagram key as in [Chapter 11](./11-patterns-of-play.md).

---

## 1. The three orientations of pressing

Every press is built on how defenders relate to opponents:

| Orientation | Each defender marks… | Strength | Weakness |
|-------------|----------------------|----------|----------|
| **Man-oriented** | a specific opponent | suffocating, disrupts build-up | rotations drag you out of shape; one beaten duel = a free man everywhere behind |
| **Zone-oriented** | a space / passing lane | keeps shape, hard to disorganise | can be passive; a free man in a "seam" between zones |
| **Ball-oriented (hybrid)** | the area around the ball, man-marking nearby | compresses the ball-side, modern default | the *far* side is deliberately left open — vulnerable to switches |

Most elite presses are **ball-oriented hybrids**: man-mark tightly near the ball, zonally
cover away from it, and leave the far flank as the "safe" concession.

---

## 2. The press by height (recap + detail)

```
   THEIR GOAL ──────────────────────────────── YOUR GOAL
   [ HIGH PRESS ]   [ MID-BLOCK ]   [ LOW BLOCK ]
   engage in        engage at        engage in
   their third      halfway          your third
```

- **High press** — highest reward (win it near their goal), highest risk (space in behind
  your line). Needs pace at the back and aggressive triggers.
- **Mid-block** — invite them in, then spring around halfway. The pragmatic default; lets
  you set traps with the ball in front of you.
- **Low block** — concede territory, defend the box, counter. Covered as a style in Ch. 02.

## 3. Matching the press to the build-up

The presser's job is to create **access** (a presser can reach the ball-carrier in time)
while staying **connected** (no gaps opened behind). The shape is chosen to match the
opponent's build-up numbers.

### Pressing a back four with a front two (the "curve" press)
A striker presses one centre-back on a curved run that uses his **cover shadow** to block
the pass to the pivot, steering play one way.

```
                ⌀○CB - - -X- - - ○DM   (lane shut by shadow)
                  ^
              ● FW curves to press, body angled
   ● FW2 marks the other CB / pivot
```
The curved run is the single most important pressing detail: it presses *and* defends a
passing lane at once.

### Pressing a back three (the front three steps up)
A front three can match a back three man-for-man, with the wing-backs picked up by the
pressing team's wide players or wing-backs.

```
   ●  ●  ●     <- front three press the three CBs
    ○  ○  ○
   ●WB pushes onto ○WB      ●WB pushes onto ○WB
```
*Trade-off:* matching high commits everyone — a played-through pass leaves acres behind.

### The "+1" problem
If they build with one more than you press (e.g. they drop a pivot to make a back three vs.
your front two), you cannot press man-for-man without leaving someone free. Solutions:
- **Push a midfielder up** to restore numbers (but this opens midfield), or
- **Use cover shadows** to "press 2 with 1" by blocking lanes, or
- **Drop off** into a mid-block and press later.

This single tension — *their build-up numbers vs. your pressing numbers* — is the heart of
the build-up-vs-press battle you score in the Battle Map (Ch. 10).

## 4. Press triggers (the full list)

A press is launched on a cue everyone recognises:

- **Back-pass** — the classic; the opponent is going backwards, momentum is with you.
- **Pass to a player facing his own goal** (closed body) — he can't play forward.
- **A heavy or slow touch** — the moment of poor control.
- **A bouncing or aerial ball** — hard to control, easy to win on the second ball.
- **A pass into a pre-set trap zone** — usually wide, toward the touchline.
- **A slow, square (sideways) pass** — gives pressers time to arrive together.
- **The ball travelling** (press *while the ball is in flight*) so you arrive as it does.

---

## 5. Pressing traps (engineered turnovers)

- **Touchline trap** — show the opponent the line, then collapse three players around him
  with the sideline removing his options.
- **Show inside / show outside** — body shape and angle of approach dictate the only
  available pass, funnelling play where you have cover.
- **The sprung pivot** — let them play into the pivot, then press from behind (a midfielder
  who was hiding) as a wide trap closes the escape.
- **Cover-shadow chains** — each presser's body blocks a lane, so the "open" pass is always
  the one you want them to make.

---

## 6. Playing *through* the press (the build-up team's answers)

Pressing and beating the press are two sides of one coin. A team breaks pressure by:

- **Creating a +1** (drop a pivot, invert a full-back, use the keeper) so a free man exists.
- **Baiting and switching** — invite the press one side, switch to the free far side
  (Ch. 11, pattern 3).
- **Third-man combinations** — bounce off a pressed player to reach a free one (Ch. 03).
- **The line-breaking pass into the pocket** — find the man between the lines the instant
  the press steps up and leaves space behind it.
- **The long "out" ball** — when trapped, go long to a target or into the channel for a
  winger to chase, accepting a 50/50 rather than a turnover in a fatal area.
- **Provocation (De Zerbi-style)** — deliberately hold the ball near your own box to *draw*
  the press in, then break it with one pass to access the now-vacated space behind.

> **The mental model:** a press is a bet that the build-up team can't find the free man
> before pressure arrives. Build-up is the bet that it can. Watch *who keeps winning that
> bet* — it usually decides territory, and territory decides chances.

---

## 7. Reading the press live — a checklist

1. **What height** are they pressing from, and does it change with the score?
2. **Man, zone, or hybrid?** Watch the far-side winger: tucked in = ball-oriented; glued to
   his man = man-oriented.
3. **What's the trigger** — back-pass? wide pass? — and is it working?
4. **Where's the trap** — which pass are they happy to concede?
5. **Is the press connected** or are players pressing in isolation (gaps opening behind)?
6. **How does the build-up team answer** — +1, switch, long ball, or panicked turnovers?

PPDA (Ch. 13) quantifies how intense this is; your eyes tell you whether it's *working*.
