# 15 — Case Studies

These worked examples apply the whole encyclopaedia — phases, styles, patterns, the Battle
Map, and prediction — to recurring match-up *types*. They are deliberately generic (archetypes,
not specific clubs) so the reasoning transfers to any fixture you watch.

For each: **the match-up → the tactical battle → likely flow → what decides it → how to read
it live.**

---

## Case 1 — Possession side vs. Low block

**Match-up:** Team A (positional possession, 3-2-5 in attack) vs. Team B (5-4-1 low block,
counter).

**The battle.** A wants to expand the pitch and find the free man between B's lines; B wants
to stay compact, deny the half-spaces and cut-back zone, and spring its quick striker.

**Likely flow.** A dominates the ball and territory (high possession, high field tilt) but
finds few clean chances against a packed box. The game's danger lives in **two moments**:
A's patience breaking it down, and B's counters when A loses the ball high.

**What decides it:**
- A's **rest defence** — if A commits too many forward and loses the ball, B's counter is
  lethal. Two secure players + a screen is the difference.
- A's **set pieces and crosses** — often the only way through a true low block.
- B's **discipline and outlet** — can the lone striker hold the ball to relieve pressure?

**Read it live:** count B's defenders in the box (5+4 = very hard centrally → A must go
wide/set pieces). Watch A's reaction to losing the ball: counterpress or scramble? When A
scores, it's usually from a switch-to-isolate (Ch. 11 #5), a cut-back, or a set piece.

**Prediction tier:** A favoured to control; **Lean**, not Lock — low-block games are
low-event and one B counter or set piece flips them.

## Case 2 — Gegenpressing side vs. Build-up side

**Match-up:** Team A (high press, gegenpress, 4-3-3) vs. Team B (patient build-up, plays out
from the back).

**The battle.** A tries to suffocate B's first pass and win the ball high; B tries to "play
through the press" and access the space *behind* A's aggressive line.

**Likely flow.** Frantic, high-tempo, lots of transitions. Whoever wins the **build-up-vs-press
bet** (Ch. 12) controls the game. If B keeps finding the free man and breaking the first
line, A's high line becomes a liability (space in behind). If A's triggers keep forcing
turnovers, B never settles and A racks up high-quality chances.

**What decides it:**
- B's **press resistance** — a composed keeper and a pivot who can receive under pressure.
- A's **line height vs. B's runners** — one ball over the top into space can punish the press.
- **Fitness and game state** — gegenpressing fades; the press is most dangerous early.

**Read it live:** watch B's first two passes out of the back. Clean = B will control;
rushed/long every time = A's press is winning. Watch the space behind A's defence on B's
turnovers — that's where B's goals come from.

**Prediction tier:** depends entirely on B's press resistance — a genuine **coin-flip / Lean**
that can be read within 15 minutes by watching the build-up battle.

## Case 3 — Direct/vertical side vs. High line

**Match-up:** Team A (direct, target man, vertical) vs. Team B (possession, very high line).

**The battle.** A bypasses midfield to attack the space behind B's high line and win second
balls around the target man; B wants the ball and is willing to defend a big space behind.

**Likely flow.** A concedes possession happily and looks to turn B around with long, direct
balls and set pieces. B will have the ball but is repeatedly exposed to balls in behind and
second-ball scrambles.

**What decides it:**
- B's **defensive line management** — the offside trap vs. A's runners; one mistimed step =
  a clear chance.
- A's **second-ball control** — the midfield must win the knockdowns from the target man.
- **Set pieces** — A's direct style usually wins more of them.

**Read it live:** watch B's centre-backs' starting position and how they handle the first
long ball. Watch who wins the second ball around the target man — that team controls the
middle third.

**Prediction tier:** if B's line is genuinely elite (pace + timing), B controls; if not, A's
plan is high-variance and dangerous — **Lean A as the upset**.

## Case 4 — Two possession sides (mirror match)

**Match-up:** Two positional-play teams, both want the ball.

**The battle.** Someone has to defend. The game is decided by **who presses better** (forces
the other into long balls) and **who has the individual edge** in the key 1v1s.

**Likely flow.** A chess match of build-up vs. press at both ends. Possession is shared;
the difference is *quality* of chances and which side's stars win their duels.

**What decides it:**
- **Pressing quality** — whoever disrupts the other's build-up gains the territory.
- **Qualitative superiority** — the elite winger or No. 10 who wins a 1v1 the structure
  can't.
- **Set pieces and one moment** — tight games often turn on a single error or piece of
  magic.

**Read it live:** ignore possession %; watch field tilt and who forces whom backwards. The
team whose build-up keeps getting pressed into long balls is quietly losing.

**Prediction tier:** **Lean** toward the side with the better press *and* the better
match-day individuals — but expect a tight, low-margin game.

---

## How to use the case studies

1. **Classify your fixture** into the nearest archetype above (or a blend).
2. **Lift the "what decides it" factors** straight into your Battle Map (Ch. 10) and
   worksheet (Ch. 16).
3. **Pre-commit to what you'll watch** ("the build-up battle", "A's rest defence") so you
   read the live game with a hypothesis instead of just following the ball.

> Most real matches are a **blend** of these archetypes (e.g. "a gegenpress side that goes
> direct when it can't press"). Recognising the components lets you reason about any
> combination.
