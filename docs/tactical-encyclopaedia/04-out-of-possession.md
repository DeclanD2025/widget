# 04 — Out-of-Possession Phase

Defending is not the absence of a plan — it is a plan. This chapter covers how organised
teams deny space, win the ball, and protect their goal.

---

## 1. Where do you defend? The three blocks

A team chooses **how high** to defend. This is the single biggest defensive decision.

| Block | Where the front line engages | Intent | Risk |
|-------|------------------------------|--------|------|
| **High press** | In the opponent's defensive third | Win the ball high, near their goal | Space in behind your high line |
| **Mid-block** | Around the halfway line | Control central space, invite then pounce | Balanced — the default for many sides |
| **Low block** | In your own defensive third | Deny space near goal, defend the box | Cede territory and possession |

A team may switch blocks by game state — press when chasing a goal, drop into a low block
to protect a lead.

---

## 2. Pressing — winning the ball on purpose

### Press triggers (when to spring)
Pressing is not constant sprinting; it is **coordinated** and launched on cues:
- a **back-pass** (the opponent is going backwards — pounce),
- a **poor or heavy touch**,
- a **pass to a player facing his own goal** (closed body),
- a **sideways/slow pass** that gives time to arrive,
- a pass into a **pre-set trap zone** (e.g. the touchline).

### Pressing traps
Defenders *invite* the opponent into bad areas:
- **Show inside / show outside** — angle the press so the only easy pass goes where you
  want (e.g. into the touchline).
- **The touchline as an extra defender** — force play wide where the sideline removes half
  the passing options, then swarm.
- **Cover shadow** — a presser positions his body to block the passing lane behind him,
  defending two opponents with one player.

### Pressing as a unit
A press only works if it is **connected**: the front presser sets the trap, the line behind
steps up to compress space, and the far side tucks in. A single player pressing alone just
opens a gap — "pressing in isolation is suicide".

---

## 3. Marking schemes

- **Zonal marking** — defenders are responsible for *space*; they mark whoever enters their
  zone. Strong structurally, can be exploited by movement and blockers.
- **Man-marking** — each defender tracks a specific opponent. Tight and disruptive, but
  movement and rotations can drag the whole structure out of shape.
- **Hybrid** — the modern norm: zonal base with man-oriented pressing in key areas (e.g.
  man-mark the pivot, zonal elsewhere).

---

## 4. Compactness — the core of organised defending

- **Horizontal compactness** — narrow distances side-to-side, protecting the centre and
  conceding only the wide areas.
- **Vertical compactness** — short distances between the defensive line and the midfield
  line (ideally ~30–35 m of pitch), denying the "between the lines" space attackers crave.
- **The trade-off:** you cannot defend everywhere. Compact teams concede space *somewhere*
  (usually wide, or in front of the block) and dare the opponent to hurt them from there.

### Defending key zones
- **The half-spaces and between the lines** — deny the No. 10 the pocket; the pivot and
  centre-backs must "pass on" runners and step to receivers.
- **The cut-back zone** — the most dangerous low cross. A defender (often the far-side
  full-back or a midfielder) must protect the penalty spot, not ball-watch.
- **Crosses and the back post** — attack the ball, mark the back-post runner, and pick up
  the **second ball** at the edge of the box.

---

## 5. The Defensive Line

- **High line** — squeezes the pitch and supports a high press, but is vulnerable to balls
  in behind and demands pace and a good offside trap.
- **Deep line** — safe behind, but invites pressure and concedes the edge of the box for
  shots.
- **The offside trap** — stepping up in unison to play attackers offside; high-reward,
  high-risk, and dependent on flawless coordination and a referee/VAR's call.

---

## 6. Rest Defence — security while you attack

"Rest defence" is the shape of the players *behind the ball* while your team attacks. It is
how you survive losing the ball.

- A common scheme is **2-3** or **3-2**: two or three defenders plus a screen of midfielders
  positioned to stop a counter the instant possession is lost.
- Good rest defence means a turnover meets immediate pressure and cover, not an open field.
- It is the bridge between the in-possession and defensive-transition phases (Chapter 05).

> **Watch for it:** when a team attacks, glance at who stays home. A team with three
> committed forwards and only two players in rest defence is gambling — and tells you where
> the game's danger lies on the counter.
