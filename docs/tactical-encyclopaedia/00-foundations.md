# 00 — Foundations & First Principles

Everything else in this encyclopaedia is built on this chapter. If formations are the
*nouns* of football and styles are the *sentences*, this chapter is the *grammar*.

---

## 1. The Four Phases of Play

Football is not one game but four, switching many times a minute. Every tactical idea
lives inside one of these phases:

1. **In possession (attacking organisation)** — you have the ball and are organised.
   Goal: progress it and create high-value chances while staying secure.
2. **Out of possession (defending organisation)** — they have the ball and you are
   organised. Goal: protect your goal, control space, and win the ball back where you want.
3. **Attacking transition** — you have *just* won the ball. The opponent is briefly
   disorganised. Goal: exploit that 3–5 second window before they re-set.
4. **Defensive transition** — you have *just* lost the ball. You are briefly
   disorganised. Goal: either win it back instantly (counterpress) or delay and re-set.

A **fifth phase**, set pieces, is treated separately (Chapter 06) because it is rehearsed,
static-start, and now decides a large share of goals.

> **Key idea:** Most goals are scored in *transition* and from *set pieces*, not from
> settled possession. A team's identity is defined as much by how it behaves in the
> two-to-five seconds after a turnover as by what it does with the ball.

## 2. The Five Reference Points

When you watch a passage of play, judge it against these five variables. They are the
"physics" of the game.

| Reference point | Question to ask | Example |
|-----------------|-----------------|---------|
| **Space** | Where is the free space, and who controls it? | A winger holding width stretches the back line |
| **Time** | How fast is the action vs. how fast can the opponent react? | A first-time pass beats a recovering defender |
| **Numbers** | Is it an overload, equality or underload here? | 3 attackers vs. 2 defenders on the right |
| **Position** | What is the body shape / passing options of the player on the ball? | An open body lets you switch play; a closed one forces backward |
| **Individual quality** | Can a player simply beat the structure? | A dribbler turning a 1v1 into a 2v1 |

Good tactics manufacture **favourable combinations** of these — e.g. creating *space* +
*time* + a *numerical overload* for a *quality* player in a good *position*.

## 3. The Pitch as a Grid

Tactics are spatial. Train yourself to see the pitch as zones, not grass.

### Thirds (vertical, goal-to-goal)
- **Defensive third** — build-up and safety. Mistakes here are punished hardest.
- **Middle third** — progression and control. Where most of the game is "fought".
- **Attacking third (final third)** — creation and finishing. Where chances are made.

### The five vertical channels (sideline to sideline)
```
| Left  | Left      | Central | Right     | Right |
| wing  | half-space|         | half-space| wing  |
```
- **Wings** — width, crossing zones, 1v1s, stretching the defence.
- **Half-spaces** — the most valuable channels in modern football. A player here can
  pass forward, dribble inside, or shoot, and is hard to mark because he sits between a
  full-back and a centre-back.
- **Central channel** — congested but the most direct route to goal; **Zone 14** (central,
  just outside the box) is the prime playmaking square.

### Key target areas
- **Zone 14** — the creative hot-spot at the top of the box, centrally.
- **The half-space pockets** — between the lines, where No. 10s and mezzalas receive.
- **The cut-back zone** — the area near the byline from which low balls are pulled back
  to the penalty spot, the highest-value assist type in the game.

> **Rule of thumb:** width is used to *create* space; the half-spaces and centre are used
> to *exploit* it.

## 4. Principles of Play

These are the universal "laws" coaches teach. Every action should serve one of them.

### Attacking principles
- **Penetration** — play forward when you can; threaten the goal.
- **Support** — give the ball-carrier near options (depth and angles).
- **Mobility** — movement to disturb markers and create passing lanes.
- **Width** — stretch the opponent horizontally to open central gaps.
- **Creativity / improvisation** — the individual spark that breaks an organised block.

### Defending principles
- **Pressure** — close the ball to deny time and force errors.
- **Cover** — a second defender behind the presser, in case he is beaten.
- **Balance** — defenders on the far side protecting space and covering switches.
- **Compactness** — short distances between players, horizontally and vertically.
- **Control / restraint (delay)** — don't dive in; slow the attack and stay on your feet.

## 5. Superiorities — the goal of all positioning

Positional play and most modern coaching aim to manufacture one of three "superiorities":

- **Numerical superiority** — more players than opponents in a zone (e.g. 3v2 in build-up).
- **Positional superiority** — players located where the opponent cannot easily defend
  them (e.g. a man free between the lines, even at 1v1).
- **Qualitative superiority** — your best player isolated against a weaker opponent in
  space (e.g. an elite winger 1v1 on the touchline).

If a team has none of the three in a given zone, the ball should be moved to a zone where
it does. That single idea explains most build-up and switch-of-play decisions.

---

## 6. Width, Depth and Compactness — the constant tension

- A team **in possession** wants to be **big**: maximum width and depth to stretch the
  opponent and open gaps.
- A team **out of possession** wants to be **small**: compact, denying space between and
  behind its lines.

Almost every tactical battle is one team trying to *expand* the pitch and the other trying
to *shrink* it. Learn to see this and you can read any match.
